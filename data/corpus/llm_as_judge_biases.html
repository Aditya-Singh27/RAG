Biases in LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation Systems

Using large language models as automated judges offers scalable evaluation, but models exhibit well-documented systematic biases that distort quality scores if left unmitigated.

1. Position Bias in Pairwise Evaluation

When presenting two model outputs (Model A vs Model B) to a judge, judges frequently favor the first candidate (Option A) due to primacy effects, or occasionally the second candidate (Option B) due to recency effects.

Mitigation: Run every evaluation pair twice with swapped order: Order 1 (A then B) and Order 2 (B then A). Measure the position flip rate. Require both evaluations to agree, or compute the average score across both orientations.

2. Verbosity and Length Bias

Judges inherently correlate response length with thoroughness and quality. A verbose response filled with padding or repetitive elaboration often scores higher than a concise, direct answer.

Mitigation: Define explicit rubrics that penalize unsupported elaboration. Use adversarial probes (verbose-but-incorrect vs terse-and-correct) to validate that the judge rewards precision over token volume.

3. Self-Enhancement Bias

LLM judges consistently assign higher ratings to outputs produced by their own model family (e.g. GPT judging GPT, Gemini judging Gemini) due to shared token distributions and stylistic habits.

Mitigation: Strictly decouple the judge model family from the generator model family (e.g. Gemini judging Llama 3.2, or an ensemble of independent models).

4. Sycophancy and Style Bias

Judges can be deceived by an authoritative, polished, or overly apologetic tone, overlooking factual falsehoods.

Mitigation: Force structured per-criterion grounding with separate reasoning steps before assigning numerical scores.
